## Professional guidance

For new or restyled decks without a client template, follow the house style in `.agents/design.md`.

### Rules

- Work from the supplied deck and its masters and layouts. Do not rebuild slides from screenshots or restyle beyond the request.
- In an existing deck, everything you add or change matches the deck's own fonts, sizes, colors, layouts, and table and chart treatment, even where they differ from the house style.
- Keep text, tables, and charts native, and use raster images only for source imagery or visuals that cannot stay native.
- Preserve hidden slides, speaker notes, comments, sections, and linked or embedded workbooks.
- Add no corner tags, eyebrow labels, or footer text beyond page numbers, source notes, and markings that the template, the user, or the genre calls for, and never join labels with a middle dot (·) or another separator glyph.
- Do not frame content in cards or callouts with a one-sided accent border.

### The presentation system

An existing deck carries its slide size, theme fonts and colors, master and layout assignments, placeholder geometry, recurring guides, and footer, page-number, confidentiality, and source treatments. Use the correct existing layout rather than placing objects on a blank slide, and fix template defects only where they block the requested result.

### Building a net-new deck

Write one small layout module before any slide code. It defines the margins, a column grid, the title, body, and source zones, and the type roles from `.agents/design.md` as named constants, and every slide places its objects through it. Every content slide carries an action title of one or two short sentences held to two lines, such as "HBM is sold out through 2026. Advanced packaging now sets the pace of supply." The title sits in the same position at the same size on every slide, and the body starts at the same height whether the title takes one line or two. For a deck built in code, this module does the work a slide master would, and it keeps zones, sizes, and spacing identical from slide to slide. Drift starts wherever slide code passes a literal coordinate or font size.

Place parallel content on the grid. Boxes in a row share a top edge, a width, and a height, with equal gutters between them, and their positions come from the grid rather than typed coordinates. A row is as tall as its fullest box. Blocks stacked in a column follow their content with equal gaps between them, rather than sitting at fixed positions. Stretching boxes to fill the slide leaves half-empty panels, so let the row end where its content ends and give the remaining space to the next element.

Set plain text boxes to zero internal padding, top anchoring, and no autofit, so text starts exactly on the grid edge and aligns with the rules, charts, and tables around it. Everything on a slide aligns by its visible edge, which is why text boxes carry no padding and the bundled icons come cropped to their artwork. A container is different: content inside a filled or bordered box sits inset from every edge by one padding, typically 0.20 in and the same in every container of the deck, and aligns to that inner edge. Text touching a border reads as a mistake, so either set the shape's text margins to that padding or place the content inside it at that inset. python-pptx anchors autoshape text to the middle by default, which leaves the headings of adjacent boxes at different heights.

Consulting slides build structure from a few quiet devices used the same way on every slide. Each group of content gets a header, either bold text over a 0.75 pt accent rule or a solid Ultramarine 700 bar with white text, and groups side by side are separated by a white gutter. When two groups differ in kind, such as findings beside implications or the analysis beside an example, set the second in a flat Mist 100 panel or divide them with a hairline vertical rule. Thin gray rules between rows, small filled circles carrying white numerals for a sequence, and one accent-colored bar among neutral ones to mark the point of a chart cover most other needs. A slide of paragraphs under a title usually wants one of these devices, and a slide that uses four at once has too many.

Icons are rare in working consulting decks. Use them only for a row of three to five parallel categories, such as the pillars of a framework, where a visual anchor helps the reader scan. The Lucide line set is installed with this skill: search `.agents/icons/index.tsv` by name or tag. Each icon is a transparent PNG in Ultramarine 700, cropped to its visible artwork on every side and saved at one shared resolution, so `add_picture` without a width or height places every icon at the same scale, about a third of an inch across for a typical icon. To change that size, scale every icon by the same factor, since fitting each one to a fixed box would give them different line weights. Because each image is exactly its artwork, it can be aligned by its left edge, by its center, or by its middle against the first line of a heading beside it, and a row of icons above headings sits on one shared bottom line so every gap to the heading is equal. Give every item in the row an icon or none of them. For white icons on a dark field or a template's accent color, run `python ".agents/icons/recolor.py" NAME RRGGBB OUT.png`. In an existing deck that has its own icon style, match that style instead.

A consulting slide can carry a lot of information, but only what proves its title. On the occasion that a slide is meant to communicate a topic in detail, a denser slide may be warranted. A dense slide carries its information in separated groups and clear information hierarchy, so a glance shows the major components before the eye moves into any one of them. Be thoughtful about constructing these slides with visual balance and structure, so they are not cluttered and difficult to view. Even if there is a lot of content, information should be cleanly placed. Not every slide is word heavy in consulting, and some other slides carry information with key charts, tables, and diagrams, sometimes with sparse commentary explaining only the most important bits.

Spacing separates the groups, and it follows the hierarchy: a heading sits close to its own text, parallel groups are separated by a wider gap, and the title, body, and source zones by the widest, such as 0.10 in, 0.30 in, and at least 0.40 in from the slide spacing scale in `.agents/design.md`. The same relationship gets the same gap on every slide. No two elements touch, so arrows, chevrons, markers, and icons keep at least 0.10 in from their neighbors. Content stays inside its zone, and text that would run into the title or source zone is cut, split, or moved to another slide.

### Build the story at slide level

Give each analytical slide one governing message. In a new deck, use a title that states the finding or implication, not just the topic. Read alone and in order, the action titles tell the deck's whole storyline, and a title that only names a topic breaks it. A slide added to an existing deck should follow that deck's title style. As you construct the storyline, think about its horizontal logic: the deck may need required sections, or a run of parallel slides, such as three of a kind, that the reader should recognize as a set. Add that structure only when it genuinely helps the reader.

Plan each slide from its title down before placing anything, so that it has vertical logic: the title states the claim, the group headers read together as the argument for it, and the content under each header proves that header. Plan within the body zone only, since the source zone below it stays reserved on every slide for sources and the page number. Decide which element proves the title and give it the largest share of the body, often half to two-thirds of the width, then arrange the supporting elements around it in reading order, left to right and top to bottom. The groups are mutually exclusive and together cover the claim, and a slide reads best with three or four at most. Parallel groups share space equally and repeat one internal structure with similar amounts of text. Set each group's position on the grid before writing its content, and when planned content will not fit its space at the fixed type sizes, cut or split it at that stage rather than squeezing the gaps later. Leftover space collects at the bottom of the body zone, above the source zone, rather than spreading into uneven gaps between groups. Hierarchy comes first from position and space, then from type size and weight, and only then from rules and color.

Match the body's structure to the content. A table compares several items across the same attributes, and a list of sentences does not belong in one. Aligned text columns, each under a short heading, suit two to four parallel points. A chart beside a short takeaway column suits data whose shape is the point, and a bridge or reconciliation table shows how one figure becomes another. A process or dependency flow is for a real sequence, and a 2×2 matrix is for two genuine dimensions. Reach for a table only when the content has that shape, since a table on every slide reads as a default. A new deck's cover contains only the title, an optional one-line subtitle, the date, the client or author when relevant, and a logo placed top right when one is supplied. Anything else on a cover is noise. A section divider carries only the context needed to orient the audience.

Avoid a universal title/subtitle/three-cards layout. One dominant visual or analytical structure per slide is usually stronger than four small panels. Keep the footer and source area quiet. In the house style, section dividers may use an Ultramarine 900 field, while analytical slides usually stay on Paper or Warm paper for contrast and printability.

### Common traps

- Saving through a library that cannot round-trip a feature, such as embedded objects, linked workbooks, or complex SmartArt, can silently drop it, even when the only change is opening and saving the file.
- Text that fits by object coordinates can still overflow once fonts are substituted, so geometry checks are warnings rather than visual proof.
- A full worksheet pasted onto a slide is unreadable at projected size.
- Stacking emphasis on one item, such as bold, blue, fill, and outline together, flattens the hierarchy it was meant to create.
- Titles that wrap after an article or leave one hanging word read as unfinished.
- Source notes and footers drift outside the safe area or collide with content on slides that were edited separately.
- Screen readers follow object order, so a slide that looks right can read out of order, and autogenerated alt text is often wrong.
- Meaning carried only by color disappears in print and for color-blind readers.
- Bulk operations can unhide hidden slides or change slides outside the request.
- New python-pptx autoshapes and connectors take their effects from the theme through a `p:style` element, and the default theme draws a drop shadow. `shadow.inherit = False` writes an empty effect list that LibreOffice ignores, so rules and boxes still render with a shadow. Removing the `p:style` element, after setting fill, line, and text color explicitly, removes the shadow in every renderer. This applies to every autoshape, including the ovals used as numbered markers, since nothing in the house style carries a shadow.
- Integer shape codes are easy to misremember. `13` is a cylinder rather than a right arrow, so use named constants such as `MSO_SHAPE.RIGHT_ARROW`.
- Source notes and footnotes outlive the content they describe, so a note can keep referring to a column or chart that a later revision removed.

### Visual review

For a new or restyled deck, an overview of all slides shows rhythm, blank slides, inconsistent margins, and unexpected shifts. Then read the slides that matter at a resolution where body text and sources are legible, which is about 150 dpi for a standard 16:9 slide. A 70 dpi overview cannot show shadows, stray glyphs, or a few points of misalignment. At the higher resolution, look for elements that touch or overlap, text that runs past its zone, and gaps that differ for the same relationship. For a scoped edit, view the changed slides and any slide whose layout the edit could affect. Without image input, do not describe rendering as visual verification, and say that visual review was not possible. Render slides as the pdf skill describes.

### Examples

A weak executive slide has the title “Revenue Overview”, four equal cards (Background / Analysis / Risks / Next Steps), twelve bullets that repeat the same variance in three places, a decorative donut comparing two periods, the footer “Source: internal analysis”, and a corner label reading “Confidential · Draft”. A stronger one is titled “Q3 revenue finished 6% below plan as two enterprise renewals moved to October”, gives two-thirds of the slide to monthly actual-versus-plan columns with the September gap and October timing shift annotated, uses the remaining third for a three-row bridge (timing, churn, expansion) and one decision box naming the cash-planning implication, and cites “Source: CRM renewal schedule as of 2026-09-25 and finance plan v4, in US$ millions”. It has one message, one visual comparison, one reconciliation, and one implication.

A weak edit to a supplied deck rebuilds the three requested slides in a new blue template, pastes screenshots of the original charts, deletes hidden slides, and reports that the deck was modernized. A stronger edit retains the supplied master and slide size, uses the nearest layouts, updates native chart data and message titles, preserves hidden and reference slides, views the changed slides and any slide their layout affects, and reports the scoped changes and any unresolved source gap.
